# Robert McKee, *Story* (distilled for treatment work)

Source: the writer-supplied text of *Story: Substance, Structure, Style and the
Principles of Screenwriting*. This file paraphrases it. Short quotes are attributed.

McKee's framing: *principles, not rules; forms, not formulas; archetypes, not
stereotypes.* An archetypal story takes a universal human experience and wraps it in a
specific, culture-rich expression. A stereotype is a narrow experience in
stale, generic dress.

## Story values and events
- **Story value**: a binary quality of human experience that can reverse its charge:
  alive/dead, love/hate, freedom/slavery, truth/lie, loyalty/betrayal,
  justice/injustice, courage/cowardice.
- **Story event**: a meaningful change in a character's life situation, expressed
  through a value and achieved through **conflict**.
- **Scene**: action through conflict, in more or less continuous time and
  space, that **turns** at least one value perceptibly. *A scene that doesn't
  turn is a non-event.* Each outline line should name its turn (`+ → –`).
- **Beat**: an exchange of action/reaction. Beats build scenes, scenes build
  sequences (a moderate turn), and sequences build acts (a major turn, the act climax).

## The gap
A character takes an action expecting a useful reaction from the world. Instead
the world reacts differently, more powerfully, or antagonistically. The **gap between
expectation and result** forces a riskier action next. Story lives in that gap.
For each key beat ask: *what did they expect, and what happened instead?*

## Protagonist
- Wilful and active, with a **conscious object of desire**. The strongest
  have a contradictory **unconscious desire** as well.
- Has the capacity to pursue the desire to the end of the line. Must have
  at least a chance of getting it.
- Empathetic, which is not the same as likeable. Look for "the centre of good": something in
  them the audience can identify with.
- **True character is revealed in choices under pressure.** The greater the pressure, the
  deeper the revelation. Characterisation (surface traits) is not character.
- Arc: the best work reveals deep character and then changes it, for better or worse.

## Inciting Incident
- It radically upsets the balance of forces in the protagonist's life. It must be on
  screen, it must happen to or be caused by the protagonist, and it arouses the
  **desire to restore balance**.
- It plants the **Major Dramatic Question** and projects an image of the
  **Obligatory Scene** (the Crisis): the confrontation the audience knows it
  must see before the story ends.
- Place it as soon as possible, but not before the audience is ready to care.
- McKee: the hardest scene to write is the last act's Climax. The second hardest
  is the Inciting Incident, which gets rewritten more than any other.

## Act design
- **Progressive complications**: escalate. Each step demands more willpower
  and risk, and must move past points of no return.
- **Law of diminishing returns**: repeating the same kind of beat weakens it.
  Escalate or vary. Never retread.
- Complicate on three levels: inner conflict, personal (family, lovers,
  friends) and extra-personal (institutions, society, environment).
- **Midpoint / mid-act climax** keeps a long middle from sagging.
- Avoid a **false ending** in the penultimate act unless it's designed as a feint.

## Progression and the values debate
- Progressions move between the positive and negative charges of the
  values at stake.
- **Idea vs. Counter-Idea**: sequence by sequence, the positive and negative
  versions of the theme argue with each other, building until they collide at Crisis.
  One wins at Climax and becomes the Controlling Idea.

## Controlling Idea
- One sentence: **value + cause**. It names the value's charge at the final climax and the chief reason
  it got there. "Justice is restored *because* the protagonist is more clever than
  the criminal."
- Derive it **from the climax**, working backward. Don't dictate it in advance.
- It is a filter: it tells you which scenes, clues and methods belong in *this* story.
- Endings are up (idealistic), down (pessimistic) or **ironic** (positive and
  negative at once). Ironic endings are hardest and often the most memorable.
- Not didactic: the audience should feel the idea through the events.

## The Principle of Antagonism
> "A protagonist and his story can only be as intellectually fascinating and
> emotionally compelling as the forces of antagonism make them."

- The sum of all forces against the protagonist (inner, personal, institutional,
  environmental) should make them an **underdog** at the Inciting Incident.
- **Ladder of negation** for the central value:
  **Positive → Contrary → Contradictory → Negation of the Negation**.
  Example: justice → unfairness → injustice → tyranny ("might makes right").
  Love → indifference → hate → self-hatred, or hate disguised as love.
  Truth → white lie → lie → self-deception. Freedom → restraint → slavery →
  slavery perceived as freedom.
- Stories that stop at the contradictory feel like TV procedurals. Great stories reach the
  negation of the negation.

## Crisis, Climax, Resolution
- **Crisis** = decision. It is the protagonist's last action, the Obligatory Scene: facing
  the most focused forces of antagonism. It must be a **true dilemma**: a choice
  between irreconcilable goods, the lesser of two evils, or both at once.
  The decision is the deepest revelation of character and makes the central value plain.
- **Climax** = the action that decision leads to. It is absolute and irreversible. If you can open the
  gap between expectation and result *once more* inside the climax, the ending
  can become majestic ("a Climax built around a Turning Point is the most satisfying").
- An ending should feel **inevitable and unexpected**.
- **Resolution**: show the spread of effects, close subplots, and give the audience a
  moment to breathe. Keep it short.
- **Deus ex machina** (outside rescue at the end) is forbidden. **Coincidence**
  may start a story early, but it must turn into meaning. It must never resolve the ending.

## Audience interest
- **Curiosity** (intellectual: what happens, why) and **concern** (emotional:
  empathy, values at stake).
- Three relationships: **Mystery** (audience knows less than the characters), **Suspense** (knows
  the same), and **Dramatic irony** (knows more). Pick each sequence's mode on purpose.

## Exposition
"Dramatise it" and "convert exposition to ammunition": reveal backstory when a character
uses it in conflict to get what they want. Hold back the biggest reveals for turning points.

## Setups and payoffs
Every payoff needs a setup. Every setup needs a payoff, or it's a false promise.
Turning points often turn backward and recharge earlier scenes with new meaning.

## Genre
Genres carry **conventions and obligatory scenes** the audience expects. Know the
genre's promises, then meet them in an unexpected way. Mixing genres is fine, but
each one must keep its promises.

## Composition
Unity (the inciting incident must make the climax possible), variety, pacing
(alternate tension and release), rising intensity, and **transitions** that
link the end of one scene to the start of the next by an image, a contrast, or a value.

## A writer's method, and why it matters for this skill
McKee's inside-out method: **step-outline** (one or two sentences per scene, how it
builds and turns, and on the back of the card, its structural job) → **pitch it**
(it must hook, hold, and move a listener in about ten minutes) → **treatment** → screenplay.

In his treatment, each scene becomes **present-tense, moment-by-moment
description with the subtext written in**: the true thoughts and feelings under
what is said and done. Characters "talk about" things, but there is no dialogue.

**Important tension.** McKee says the 10–12 page "treatments" passed around the
industry are really outlines with enough words for a reader to follow, and that
a working treatment runs 60–90 pages. This skill writes the **10–15 page
selling treatment** the market asks for. That makes it more important that the
outline underneath has been tested hard (the Gauntlet), and that key scenes in the
short treatment still show subtext. If the writer then wants to draft, recommend
a longer private Manual/McKee treatment before screenplay pages.

**Pitch test (adapted):** can the story, told aloud from the outline in about ten minutes,
hook at the inciting incident, hold through the progressions, and move the listener at the
climax? If not, it won't improve by getting longer.
