# The Finnish Method (distilled)

Source: the writer-supplied consolidated transcript of the Finnish Method book,
which uses the feature *Lockdown* as its worked example. This file paraphrases it.

## Core diagnosis
Most stuck writers aren't short of talent. They have a **workflow problem**: they try to do
*creation* (what happens, who, why, in what order) and *execution* (pages,
dialogue, rhythm) at the same time. The symptom is the **First-Ten-Pages
Loop**: polishing the opening because the next part hasn't been thought out.

**Fix:** separate the jobs and use a chain of small, finishable,
private documents. Each one feeds the next:

Raw Idea → Private Logline → One-Page Synopsis → Bullet Outline → The Manual → (optional Treatment) → Pages

Note: the Finnish Method treats a prose treatment as an optional detour, needed
when "a fund, producer, school, or collaborator asks for one", and
builds it **from the Manual**. This skill does exactly that, with the 10–15 page
treatment as the deliverable.

## Rules that apply at every stage
- **Forward motion beats correctness.** A bad temporary answer is better than a
  blank, because you can test it.
- **Keep it private, ugly, useful.** Once a work document is aimed at an
  audience, honest thinking stops.
- **Every document starts from the previous one.** Never face a blank page.
- **Re-enter, don't restart.** Find the level where the problem lives and fix it there.

## 1. Raw idea
Dump it in 10–15 minutes without editing. Find the **spark**: the single reason
you want to write this (an image, contrast, question, wound, or feeling). Everything else
can change. The spark is what you check changes against.
*Done when:* you can point at the part that excites you and say "that".

## Logline: Workhorse vs. Showhorse
- **Showhorse**: the commercial, sleek, public line. Write it *later*.
- **Workhorse**: private, 50–100 words, plain language. It has four parts:
  1. **Who** it's about
  2. What they **want**
  3. What's **in the way**
  4. What **makes it go** (pressure, stakes, why now)
- It is a **diagnostic**: a missing part shows where the story is soft.
  No who → no protagonist chosen. No want → a situation, not a story. No obstacle →
  not enough conflict. No engine → no pressure.
- Common failures: **mood instead of story** ("a haunting meditation on grief…");
  **plot without meaning** ("a detective investigates murders"); **clever too
  early** (clarity beats elegance); **abstract obstacle** ("her past",
  "society", "fear"). The obstacle must be able to *press*: a killer, a deadline, a
  trial, a lie about to be exposed.
- *Done when:* it tells you what's happening and why **you** care.

## 2. One-page synopsis
- The whole story on one page, start to finish, **ending included**.
- One page is the limit on how much you're allowed to avoid. A story that needs
  ten pages to be understood at this stage hasn't been understood yet.
- **Synopsis trap:** atmosphere hides missing story ("a mood board"). Use plain
  subject-verb-object sentences: *She finds the body. He lies to the police.*
- **Make up an ending now**, even the obvious one. "Open-ended" often means "I didn't decide".
- Write in **prose paragraphs, not bullets**. Bullets can sit in order
  without proving causality. Prose demands the causal link.
- **Paragraph pull:** each paragraph opens a question and answers it, and the
  answer opens the next question. *Therefore*, not *and then* (Parker & Stone).
- If it bores *you*, that is a momentum problem in the story, not a style problem.
- **Read-aloud test:** if you keep saying "what I mean is…", put that explanation on the page.
- Names arrive here, because people now do specific things in a specific order.
  Details like "seven days" can change (it became five). The clock was the point.

## 3. Bullet outline (scene list)
- **One line per scene**, with function, not mood: who is in it, what happens, why it exists.
  - Weak: "Sara stands in the cold morgue, grey light…" (execution)
  - Good: "Sara walks Atli through the staged suicide, shows hesitation marks:
    the case isn't as simple as he thought." (function)
- A scene you can't state in one line has no point yet, or has too many.
- Start with the scenes you know. Pin them in place, then connect the fixed points: "How do I get
  from that situation to this one?"
- **PLACEHOLDER:** rough guess, highlighted. Never let one gap stop the outline.
- **Reorder ruthlessly.** It's cheap because nothing is written yet.
- Even quiet character scenes get reduced to function ("this changes their
  relationship so that later, when one risks something for the other, it lands").
- **Logic check:** does scene N *create* scene N+1? Could you lift it out
  and have the next scene play the same? If so, it's a dead spot.
- **Density check:** acts are roughly similar in size. If the ending is 3 rows, it's probably an
  abrupt exit, not a climax. If it takes 20 rows before the story starts, the writer is hiding in setup.
- Big sequences (heist, chase) may use several lines if each beat pushes the next.
- Block labels help (e.g. *PARTNERS*, *DOUBT*, *DAY THREE*).

## 4. The Manual
- Expand each line into 1–3 plain paragraphs. **Name** each scene (for mood) and add a
  **function line** under the name (for plot).
- Four questions per scene: **Visual** (open and close on an image) · **The in**
  (enter late, on something curious) · **Want & need** (conflicting motivations)
  · **The out** (a mini-cliffhanger into the next scene).
- **No dialogue.** If a perfect line appears, put it in [brackets] and move on.
- **Clarity bar:** could *another writer* write the scene from this? Could
  *tired future-you*?
- Watch for **vague phrases that hide unresolved turns**: "they talk until they agree",
  "she figures out a way in", "the relationship shifts". Ask how, who gives
  ground, and who leaves with more power. Then decide.
- Skip stubborn scenes and come back to them once the scenes around them exist.

## 5. Pages (for later; not this skill's deliverable)
Write vertically, don't fix backwards (use FIX LATER notes), and stop mid-scene.
When something breaks: structure → outline, lost thread → synopsis, scene
purpose → manual, "what is this film?" → logline.

## How this skill uses it
Stages 0–4 follow the Finnish documents. The Manual's discipline (visual in/out,
want vs. need, function line, no dialogue, clarity bar) shapes how key scenes
are written *inside* the 10–15 page treatment.
