# Michael Arndt, "Endings: The Good, the Bad, and the Insanely Great" (distilled)

Source: the writer-supplied transcript of Arndt's lecture (Arndt wrote *Little Miss
Sunshine*). This file paraphrases it. Arndt stresses it is *descriptive, not
prescriptive*: "tools, not rules". He also says these ideas don't generate a
first draft. They help you shape one once inspiration has given you material.

## Endings
- **Bad:** positive but predictable.
- **Good:** positive and surprising.
- **Insanely great:** positive, surprising and **meaningful**.
- Meaning comes from **values**: competing visions of how to live. The climactic
  *two minutes* reveal the film's meaning. (McKee, quoted by Arndt: the more meaning,
  the more emotion.)

## Three sets of stakes
Each set is **binary, pass/fail**.

| Stakes | Typical forms | Star Wars | The Graduate | Little Miss Sunshine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **External** (world) | survival, money, contest, position | survival of the Rebellion | Ben's future: will it be "different"? | will Olive win the pageant? |
| **Internal** (emotional) | romantic love, parent–child, friendship, self-respect | Luke's call to greatness | connection with Elaine | Olive's dreams and her bond with her father |
| **Philosophical** (values) | community vs. individual, authenticity vs. ideal… | altruism/connection vs. selfishness/empire | feelings vs. rules/conformity | private self (fun, freedom) vs. public self (winners/losers) |

Most films have the first two. **Most films fail or go muddy on the third.**

Each set has its **own antagonist and its own arc** (its own inciting incident,
act breaks, midpoint, climax). Star Wars: Vader (external), Uncle Owen
(internal: "you're not that special"), Han (philosophical: "it's all tricks and nonsense").

## Values drama
- **Dominant values**: pervasive in the story world, on the verge of crushing the
  **underdog values** (the scrappy misfit values). A sudden reversal lets the underdog
  values prevail. That **overturning of the story world's moral order** is the meaning.
- **Antagonist aria**: usually near the end of Act 1, someone tells the hero "kid,
  here's how the world works" and states the dominant values. (In *The
  Graduate* the real aria isn't "plastics". It's Mr Robinson's "sow a few wild oats".)
- **Mentor**: speaks the underdog values in Act 1. The mentor can be inner. Ben's mentor
  is his own feelings.
- **Judas moment**: in Act 3, the hero's closest ally betrays them *philosophically* by
  choosing the dominant values (Han leaves; Elaine's note "it would never work";
  Dwayne's "she's not a beauty queen").
- **Innocent heroes in a flawed universe**: Luke, Ben and Olive aren't the flawed
  ones. The world is, and the hero *redeems* it. (Compare a flawed hero who changes. Both work.
  Decide which one this story is.)

## Organic logic ("once upon a time")
| Fairy-tale beat | Script term | Approx. page (100 pp) | Image |
|---|---|---|---|
| Once upon a time… and every day… | Opening / status quo (plus an unresolved issue) | 1 | tanking up emotional rocket fuel |
| But then one day… | **Inciting incident** | ~10 | lighting the fuse |
| And so the hero set out… | **Act 1 break** | ~25 | blast-off: maximum rooting interest |
| Suddenly, without warning… | **Midpoint** | ~50 | rug pulled out |
| And there was no going back… | **Act 2 break** | ~75 | trap door → canoe heading for the waterfall |
| Climax | **Climax** | ~90 | do or die |

"If you can tell me your six tentpole moments, I can tell you whether you
have a good story."

Details:
- **Opening**: a world in equilibrium. The hero has a stable sense of self, of the world,
  and a *clear visual sense of their future*.
- **Inciting incident**: changes their sense of self, world and future. Often it takes away the
  thing they value most, with "insult added to injury". It creates the **global
  problem** that the climax solves.
- **Act 1 break**: after dithering, the hero seizes on a plan. Act 2 pursues a
  **local goal** that should solve the global goal (get to Redondo Beach, deliver
  R2 to Alderaan).
- **Just after Act 2 starts**: blow up the plan (Alderaan is gone; "don't date Elaine").
- **Midpoint**: setback or reversal. Stakes deepen, something hidden is revealed, and the hero
  often has to face an inner flaw.
- **Act 2 break**: the hero *achieves the local goal* and in doing so **forces
  the global stakes** (delivering the plans reveals the base; arriving at Redondo commits
  Olive to the pageant).

## The Act 3 run (pp 75–90)
1. **Nothing goes right.** Setbacks hit external, internal *and* philosophical
   stakes. In Star Wars, Obi-Wan dies (internal), the base is located (external), Han leaves
   (philosophical), and then the run gets worse.
2. **Judas moment** (philosophical betrayal by the closest ally).
3. **Kamikaze moment of commitment**: the mentor's voice returns, often heard only by
   the hero. The hero chooses the underdog values *against self-interest*. The audience
   should think "what are you doing?!"
4. **Moment of despair**: failure on all three stakes. If you paused here, no
   positive outcome would seem possible. The hero has gone to the end of their ability
   and still fallen short, which is what *earns* the reversal.
5. **Decisive act**: positive, surprising and meaningful, because it **embodies the
   underdog values** (not a speech). It flips all three stakes, logically
   linked, close together (about 45 seconds or less), in **ascending order of importance**.
   - Star Wars: Han returns (philosophical) → Luke's shot (external) → "the Force will
     be with you" (internal).
   - The Graduate: "Ben!" (internal) → "Not for me" (philosophical) → escape (external).
   - Little Miss Sunshine: the external stakes are *lost* (she won't win) while the philosophical
     stakes are won (Grandpa's values) and the internal stakes are won (Richard joins her). An ending can
     lose one set of stakes and still be insanely great.

## How this skill uses it
- Stage 1: fill in all three stakes, each with a named antagonist.
- Stage 2: values, aria, mentor, Judas, kamikaze, despair, decisive act.
- Stage 4: the six tentpoles, plus a stakes-track table showing each set's arc.
- Gauntlet C and D test all of this.
