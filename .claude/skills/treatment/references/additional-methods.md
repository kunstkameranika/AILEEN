# Additional methods (outside the supplied sources)

These come from widely taught craft texts and talks. They are summarised from general
knowledge, not from files the writer supplied. Use them as cross-checks. Where they conflict
with McKee, Arndt or the Finnish Method, those three take priority unless the writer says otherwise.

## Trey Parker & Matt Stone: "therefore / but"
Write out your beats. If you can put "and then" between two beats, the story is
broken. Every beat should connect with **therefore** (cause) or **but**
(reversal). This is the simplest causality test there is. It is Gauntlet B1.

## John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*
Seven key steps, which work as a checklist for the protagonist's line:
1. **Weakness and need.** The weakness is psychological (hurts self) and/or moral
   (hurts others). The need is what they must fix to live better.
   Often there's a **ghost**: a past wound that still haunts them.
2. **Desire**: a specific external goal that drives the plot.
3. **Opponent**: the best opponent wants the *same thing* (or competes for it) and
   attacks the hero's weakness most effectively.
4. **Plan**: how the hero means to win.
5. **Battle**: the final conflict.
6. **Self-revelation**: the hero sees the truth about themselves (psychological and
   moral). It must be earned, sudden, and new to them.
7. **New equilibrium**: higher or lower than at the start.

Also: the **moral argument**. The hero's moral weakness drives immoral actions under pressure.
The opponent's values sharpen the argument, and the ending judges it. This lines up with
Arndt's philosophical stakes and McKee's idea vs. counter-idea. Truby's
**designing principle** asks what single organising idea makes *this* telling original.
It is the "why this, told this way" for the package.

## Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (marketability lens)
- **Logline test**: (1) **irony** (a hook built into the premise), (2) a
  **compelling mental picture** (you can see the movie), (3) **audience and cost**
  (who it's for, and roughly what it costs to make), (4) a **killer title** that says what it is.
- **"What is it?"** If you can't say it in a sentence, buyers can't sell it.
  Same thing, but different.
- **Beat sheet** (cross-check only; don't force it): Opening Image · Theme Stated ·
  Set-Up · Catalyst · Debate · Break into Two · B Story · Fun & Games (the "promise
  of the premise") · Midpoint (false victory or false defeat) · Bad Guys Close In ·
  All Is Lost ("whiff of death") · Dark Night of the Soul · Break into Three · Finale ·
  Final Image (a mirror of the opening).
- **Promise of the premise**: the treatment must deliver the set-pieces the
  logline implies, mostly in the first half of Act 2. Gauntlet F2.
- **Primal**: would a caveman understand why the hero wants this (survival,
  love, protecting family, revenge, fear of death)?
- **"Save the Cat" moment**: give the audience an early reason to go with the hero.
  Not necessarily niceness. Competence, wit or suffering also work.
- Snyder's genres (structural families, not marketing genres): Monster in the
  House, Golden Fleece, Out of the Bottle, Dude with a Problem, Rites of Passage,
  Buddy Love, Whydunit, Fool Triumphant, Institutionalized, Superhero. They are handy for
  checking what the story *is* and for finding comps.

## Frank Daniel / Paul Gulino: the sequence approach
A feature runs as about **8 sequences** of 10–15 minutes. Each one is a mini-movie with its own
protagonist goal, tension, and resolution that opens the next sequence's question.
Typical spread: Act 1 = sequences A, B (B ends on the Act 1 break). Act 2 = C, D (D ends on the
midpoint), E, F (F ends on the Act 2 break). Act 3 = G (climax), H (resolution, epilogue).
- Each sequence answers a **sequence dramatic question** and ends on a turn.
- Useful for **treatment architecture**. Writing the treatment sequence by sequence
  gives each block of about 1.5 pages a shape.
- Also from Daniel: **dramatic question** (will X get Y?), **"somebody wants something
  badly and is having difficulty getting it"**, and **plant/payoff**.

## Pixar story rules (Emma Coats' widely shared list), selected
- Story spine (Kenn Adams): *Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day
  ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___.*
  Use it as a 30-second sanity test of the synopsis.
- You admire a character more for trying than for succeeding.
- What is your character good at and comfortable with? Throw the opposite at them.
- Come up with the ending before the middle. Endings are hard.
- Coincidences that get characters *into* trouble are great. Coincidences that get them *out* are cheating.
- What are the stakes? Give us a reason to root for the character. What happens if they fail?
- Why must *you* tell this story? (This feeds "why me" in the package.)
- Discount the first thing that comes to mind, and the second. Surprise yourself.

## Alfred Hitchcock: suspense vs. surprise
A bomb under the table that suddenly goes off gives 15 seconds of surprise. A bomb the audience
*knows about* gives 15 minutes of suspense. Decide what the audience knows
and when (McKee's mystery, suspense, dramatic irony). Gauntlet B6.

## Lajos Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing*: premise
A premise is *character + conflict → conclusion* ("ruthless ambition leads to its
own destruction"). It's an earlier, cruder form of McKee's Controlling Idea. It is useful
when the writer finds "value + cause" too abstract.

## Dan Harmon: story circle (for series and pilots)
You (comfort zone) → Need → Go (unfamiliar situation) → Search (adapt) → Find
→ Take (pay a heavy price) → Return → Change. This is good for checking episodic arcs and
whether a pilot's A-story completes a circle while opening the series engine.

## John Yorke, *Into the Woods*
Structure is **change**. The midpoint is often where the protagonist first glimpses
the truth or learns the lesson they need. The end of Act 2 is the worst point. The climax is where
the lesson is applied. This is a good cross-check on the internal arc.

## How to use these
- Use Parker/Stone, Pixar's spine and Snyder's logline test in every loop. They're cheap.
- Use Truby to deepen the internal and moral line when Arndt's internal or philosophical stakes are weak.
- Use Frank Daniel sequences to build the treatment's architecture.
- Use Snyder's genres and Hitchcock when choosing comps and the audience-knowledge strategy.
