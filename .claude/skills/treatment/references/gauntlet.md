# The Gauntlet

The test battery from Stage 5. Run every test, give each a verdict (**PASS / WEAK / FAIL**), cite
the evidence (the scene or line), propose a fix, and name the **fix level**
(L = logline/spark, E = ending/meaning, S = synopsis, O = outline, T = treatment
prose). Fix at that level, propagate downstream, and re-run.

Log format (`05-gauntlet.md`, one block per loop):

```
## Loop N: <date>, against: outline vN / treatment vN
| # | Test | Verdict | Evidence | Fix | Level |
|---|------|---------|----------|-----|-------|
```

Stop when no FAIL remains, after about 4 loops, or when a fix would change the spark or a
writer-owned choice. In those cases, ask the writer and offer options.

---

## A. Engine
- **A1 Four-part logline.** Who, want, obstacle and engine are all concrete. The obstacle can
  *press* (not "her past", "society", "fear").
- **A2 Active protagonist.** The protagonist makes the key choices that drive the plot,
  especially the act-break decisions and the decisive act. Fail if events happen *to* them while
  someone else solves the story. (A deliberate passive protagonist must be controlled: see McKee
  minimalism. Flag it, don't auto-fail.)
- **A3 Want vs. need.** There is a conscious goal, and an inner need that pulls against it, or a
  deliberate innocent hero who redeems a flawed world (Arndt). State which.
- **A4 Inciting incident.** It's on screen, involves the protagonist, upsets the balance, creates the
  want, and plants a Major Dramatic Question whose answer is the climax. It comes roughly in the
  first 10–15% (later only if the setup is itself gripping).
- **A5 Primal / empathy.** Is there a reason to go with the protagonist (a centre of good)? Would
  anyone understand why they want this?
- **A6 Concept hook.** "It's the only ___ film where ___." The second blank is specific to this
  material, not the genre. A sales agent could pitch it in one line that no other film on the
  slate shares. Generic premise + genre conventions = FAIL for a market treatment. The signature
  element must be fused to the conflict (the opponent created, exploits or hides behind it).
  Swappable backdrop = WEAK.

## B. Causality and logic
- **B1 Therefore/but chain.** Read the synopsis and outline, beat by beat, joined with "therefore"
  or "but". Every "and then" is a FAIL at that joint.
- **B2 Lift-out test.** Delete each scene in your head. If the next scene plays the same, the scene
  is dead. Cut it or give it a turn.
- **B3 Every scene turns a value.** Each outline line has a `+/–` turn. If several scenes in a row
  have the same charge and the same kind of beat, that's a diminishing-returns WEAK/FAIL.
- **B4 Progressive complications.** Risk and cost escalate through acts and past points of no
  return. There is no retreat to an earlier status quo without a price.
- **B5 Motivation ("why don't they just…?").** The opponent's crime must be proportional to what
  they stand to lose. A small secret behind a big crime is a FAIL. A climactic trap only works
  if the bait threatens the opponent with something only it carries. For every major choice by any major character, ask
  why now and why this rather than the obvious alternative (call the police, leave, tell the
  truth). The story must answer it, ideally on the page.
- **B6 Information logic.** Track who knows what, and when. No character acts on knowledge they
  don't have. That includes the opponent, who also acts on everything a competent opponent would
  know. Reveals are set up. For each sequence, decide on purpose whether it runs on mystery,
  suspense, or dramatic irony.
- **B7 Setups and payoffs.** Every payoff has a setup. Every major setup pays off (or is cut).
  Clues, skills, objects and rules are planted before they are needed.
- **B8 World rules and plausibility.** The internal rules (genre, magic, institutions, technology,
  law, procedure) are stated or implied, and then kept. Characters behave like competent
  professionals in their fields unless incompetence is the point.
- **B9 Time and geography.** Clocks, day counts, travel, ages and timelines are consistent. If
  there's a clock, it ticks visibly and gets shorter. Check the weekdays of real dates. Check who
  is on which side of every barrier, and which way everyone is moving. This is the most common
  FAIL that remains, so check it against the continuity sheet line by line.
- **B10 No coincidence out of trouble / no deus ex machina.** Coincidence may start trouble early.
  It never solves the ending. The same goes for the **opponent's gift**: the means of victory
  must not come from the antagonist's sudden lapse, generosity or a rule introduced late. Trace
  the decisive tool back to a scene where the protagonist earned or chose it.
- **B11 Gap test on key beats.** At each tentpole, state what the protagonist expected and what
  happened instead. If expectation equals result, it's not a turning point.
- **B12 Solve-point.** For any hidden answer (killer, secret, twist), mark the first moment an
  alert reader could solve it. If that comes well before the protagonist solves it and the
  dramatic irony isn't deliberate, FAIL. Decisive clues need a misleading first meaning. There must be at least two credible suspects, and
  the culprit's cover must hold until it's broken late. No two decisive clues may work the same way.
- **B13 Discovered, not disclosed.** Major revelations come from a character's own action in a
  scene that forces a choice. A revelation delivered by the opponent's speech or by exposition is
  a FAIL.
- **B14 Forced move.** Each climactic action, by hero or opponent, is forced by something concrete on
  the page (a deadline, a threat, a witness, a thaw) that rules out the easier alternative. If the
  character could simply do something safer, it's a FAIL.

## C. Stakes and antagonism
- **C1 Three stakes.** External, internal and philosophical are each defined as pass/fail, each
  set up in Act 1, and each with its own antagonist.
- **C2 Stakes track.** Each set of stakes moves at the tentpoles (a table in the outline). No set
  sleeps through Act 2.
- **C3 Underdog.** At the inciting incident, the sum of antagonism outweighs the protagonist's
  resources.
- **C4 Negation of the negation.** The central value's ladder is written out (positive → contrary →
  contradictory → negation of the negation) and the story reaches the last rung by Act 3.
- **C5 Opponent quality.** The main opponent competes for the same thing, or attacks the hero's
  exact weakness, and has a coherent logic of their own (their own aria).
- **C6 Midpoint.** There is a real reversal or revelation that changes the direction of the
  pursuit, deepens the stakes, or exposes a flaw. No sag.
- **C7 Act 2 break.** The local goal is achieved (or definitively lost) in a way that forces the
  global stakes into do-or-die.
- **C8 Opponent on the page.** The opponent (or the antagonistic force) takes a hostile *action*
  in the first ~10% and in every sequence after that, with a plan that adapts to the hero.
  Warnings, phone calls and reputation don't count. An opponent who appears only at the
  midpoint, or a middle with nobody pushing back, is a FAIL. So is an opponent whose only weapon
  is paper (files, photos, debts) with no irreversible act on the page by the end of Act 1.
  The leverage must still bite after each of the hero's moves.
- **C9 Burden alone.** By the end of Act 2a, no mentor, partner or competent ally can carry the
  protagonist's burden for them. The key choices of Acts 2b and 3 are theirs alone. (In a
  two-hander, treat the pair as the protagonist.)

## D. Ending
- **D1 Act 3 run.** From the Act 2 break to the climax, things keep going wrong on all three
  stakes. No easy wins.
- **D2 Judas / ally test.** Someone close to the hero embraces the dominant values (or the
  writer's equivalent pressure exists).
- **D3 Crisis dilemma.** The protagonist's final decision is a true dilemma (irreconcilable goods,
  or the lesser of two evils), taken under maximum pressure, and it reveals deep character.
- **D4 Moment of despair.** At its lowest point, no positive outcome seems possible to a reader.
- **D5 Decisive act.** It is the protagonist's own action. The opponent confessing, unmasking
  themselves, slipping up or changing their mind is a FAIL. It embodies the underdog values (or
  the counter-idea wins on purpose). It flips the stakes logically, close together, in ascending
  order. Nobody explains the theme in a speech.
- **D6 Climax quality.** It is absolute and irreversible, answers the Major Dramatic Question, and
  ideally holds one last gap between expectation and result. It is inevitable *and* unexpected.
- **D7 Meaningful ending.** It is positive (or deliberately negative/ironic), surprising, and
  meaningful. You can state a Controlling Idea (value + cause) that the climax, not dialogue,
  proves.
- **D8 Resolution.** It is brief and shows the new equilibrium. Subplots close. A final image
  mirrors or answers the opening.
- **D9 Cost.** The decisive act costs the protagonist something irreplaceable (a life,
  a bond, freedom, an identity). A clever trick that costs nothing is WEAK at best.

## E. Spark and theme
- **E1 Spark survives.** The spark from intake is visible and central in the outline and treatment.
- **E2 Idea vs. counter-idea.** Sequences alternate between the positive and negative versions of
  the theme. The argument is fair, and the counter-idea gets strong advocates.
- **E3 Unity.** Every subplot either supports, contradicts, or complicates the central idea. Cut or
  reshape any that does none of these.
- **E4 Archetype, not stereotype.** There is specific, textured world detail on top of a universal
  experience. Flag any generic settings, roles or images.

## F. Market
- **F1 Showhorse logline.** It passes Snyder's test: irony/hook, a mental picture, a clear audience,
  and a fitting title. It fits in one or two sentences.
- **F2 Genre promise.** It names the primary genre (and secondary, if any) and lists that genre's
  conventions and obligatory scenes. The treatment delivers each one, ideally in a fresh way.
  "Promise of the premise" set-pieces are present.
- **F3 Hook speed.** The first half-page sets out world, tone, protagonist and a pulling question.
  The inciting incident lands by about page 2 of the treatment.
- **F4 Comps.** 2–3 comparable titles, ideally recent (roughly the last 10 years), successful in
  this format and market, and close in tone and audience. Never invent box office or
  festival facts. Mark comps "verify" unless confirmed.
- **F5 "Why now / why me".** Relevance to the present, and the writer's connection to the material.
  Ask the writer for the second. Never invent it.
- **F6 Castable roles.** The protagonist and the antagonist(s) each have a big, active arc and
  a defining choice an actor would want to play.
- **F7 Producibility signals.** Scale, locations and set-pieces are consistent with the intended
  budget level and territory, or the gap is flagged.

## G. Prose (treatment only)
- **G1 Mood-board detector.** Search for vague or inflated phrases: *haunted by, forced to
  confront, journey, everything changes, spiral, descent, reckoning, shattering, must learn to,
  comes to terms, the truth about, unimaginable, a web of*. Each instance must sit next to a
  concrete event, or be cut.
- **G2 Vague-turn detector.** Look for "they talk", "eventually", "somehow", "finds a way",
  "realises", "the relationship shifts", "things escalate". Replace each with *what happens* and
  *who gains or loses power*.
- **G3 Read-aloud test.** No sentence needs an extra spoken explanation.
- **G4 Length and balance.** 10–15 pages (about 4,500–7,500 words). The act proportions are
  roughly 25/50/25 (Act 3 up to ~33% when the hinges live there; Act 2 at 40% or more), and Act 3
  is not compressed into a paragraph.
- **G5 Format.** Present tense, third person, CAPS on first introductions, minimal dialogue, clear
  sequence breaks.
- **G6 Subtext.** Key scenes show what characters want underneath what they do, briefly.
- **G7 Cold read.** Done by a fresh reader (a subagent given only the treatment) wherever
  possible. Self-graded cold reads miss most problems. A reader with no working files can say what happens, what the protagonist
  wants, what's at stake, and what the ending means. Note where they got bored or confused.
- **G8 Ending not withheld.** The treatment tells the ending. Sales and fund treatments that hide
  the ending usually count against the writer.
- **G9 Hinges rendered.** The 3–5 beats the climax depends on, above all the protagonist's
  defining or fatal choices, are rendered as scenes in which we watch the choice being made. A
  hinge told in one sentence of summary ("She believes it, because…"), or elided ("we don't need
  to hear it", a nod, "he has agreed"), is a FAIL.
- **G10 Opening pressure.** Page one has a threat or opponent acting, not a briefing.
  Logistics and world rules come inside scenes of conflict.
- **G11 Readable logic.** The protagonist's goal is one visible line. The climax can be pictured
  without a map. No passage stacks more than about three figures (distances, thicknesses, times).
  Plausibility that needs a manual to follow is a FAIL, even if every number checks out.

---

## Who runs it
The writer of the document runs the outline loops. The **final** Gauntlet on the treatment is
run by a fresh reader who sees only the treatment and this file (see SKILL.md Stage 7). Every
verdict cites a line *from the treatment*. Evidence that lives only in the outline doesn't count.

## Severity guidance
- **FAIL**: it breaks causality, a stake, the ending, or the spark, or a buyer would stop reading.
- **WEAK**: it works but is soft, generic, or repetitive. It's fine to carry forward if the writer accepts it.
- Fix the highest-level FAILs first. One fix at L or E level often clears several O-level FAILs.
